Workforce Attrition & Retention Analytics · Case study

22%

Product-team attrition jumped to 22%. Here’s why, and how to bring it back down.

At NimbusTech, a 1,200-person software company, people leaving Engineering, Product and Data teams added $1.8M in replacement hiring costs in a single year. I analyzed three years of workforce data to find the causes and build a costed retention plan.

01

The problem

The rest of the company held steady at about 13–14%. Product teams went from 12% to 22% in two years.

These teams build the product, so every exit means lost delivery time, roughly two months to backfill each role, and the cost of recruiting and onboarding. Replacement costs for these teams rose from $2.9M in 2024 to $4.7M in 2025.

Attrition rate = people who left during the year ÷ average headcount. Replacement cost = 50% of annual salary per leaver for product roles, at the low end of industry estimates (50–200%).

The questions I set out to answer

  1. Where is attrition concentrated?
  2. Why are people leaving?
  3. When in their time at the company do they leave?
  4. What is it costing, and what would fixing it save?
  5. Who is most at risk of leaving next?
02

What I found

Five clear causes, and they overlap. Burnout, manager issues and stalled careers together explain nearly 70% of 2025 exits from product teams.

Finding 1 · Burnout

Working more than 20 hours of overtime a month doubles the chance of leaving.

27% of heavy-overtime employees left in 2025, compared with 13% of everyone else. Product teams now average 18 overtime hours a month, almost twice the rest of the company (10). Exits citing burnout rose from 5 in 2024 to 21 in 2025, making it the top exit reason.

What it means: the 2025 release pace is not sustainable, and it is the single biggest driver.

Finding 2 · Managers

Two managers account for half of all Engineering exits.

Their teams make up 19% of Engineering but lost 28 of 56 Engineering leavers in 2025: a 47% leaving rate against 11% for other teams. Both have been managers for only about a year, and their teams report the lowest job satisfaction.

What it means: a targeted fix with fast payback. Coaching first-time managers works better than a company-wide program.

Finding 3 · Career growth

No promotion in two years makes people three times more likely to leave.

Among employees with 2+ years at the company, 20% of those not promoted recently left, against 6% of those who were. Promotions in product teams slowed sharply in 2024–25, and job satisfaction is lower in the not-promoted group (2.7 vs 3.5 out of 5).

What it means: people can’t see a path forward, so they find one somewhere else.

Finding 4 · Pay

Pay matters, but only for senior staff.

Senior employees paid 10%+ below market left at 40%, four times the rate of seniors paid at market (10%). Junior staff are almost all paid at market and still leave, so pay is not their reason. A company-wide raise would spend money where it doesn’t help.

What it means: make targeted adjustments for a small group, not across-the-board raises.

Finding 5 · First year

One in three leavers quits within their first year.

26% of employees in their first year left in 2025, almost three times the rate of people in their second year (9%). The most common reason early leavers give is their manager (9 of 25), so new hires are landing in the two struggling teams from Finding 2.

What it means: fixing those two teams also fixes onboarding. Every early exit means paying the hiring cost twice.

The costliest part: 30 of the 72 voluntary leavers were top performers (rated 4–5 out of 5). Replacing them alone costs about $1.9M.

What the data ruled out

Ruling these out saves budget for the fixes that work.

  • Commute distance16% vs 18%Long commutes (20+ miles) don’t raise exits. Commute perks won’t help.
  • Remote vs office15–18%Similar across remote, hybrid and onsite. Not a work-location issue.
  • Gender17% vs 18%No meaningful gap between men and women. A good fairness check.
03

What I recommend

Six actions in priority order. Each one targets a cause found above.

  1. Next 30 days

    Hold stay interviews with the 43 highest-risk employees

    A simple risk score (overtime, time since promotion, pay vs market, manager, tenure) flags 43 current employees, including 13 top performers. HR business partners meet each one this month.

    Owner: HR Business Partners
  2. 0–3 months

    Support the two high-attrition Engineering teams

    Coaching and skip-level check-ins for both managers, plus a first-time manager program for everyone promoted into management.

    Owner: Engineering Director + HR
  3. 0–3 months

    Put guardrails on overtime

    Flag anyone above 20 overtime hours for two months running. Plan sprints against real team capacity, and use contractors for release crunches.

    Owner: VP Engineering
  4. Next pay cycle

    Raise pay for the 15 senior staff paid furthest below market

    Raising them to 95% of market costs about $241K a year. Replacing one of them costs about $76K, so keeping just 4 of the 15 covers the cost.

    Owner: Finance + HR
  5. 3–6 months

    Publish career ladders and review promotions every 12 months

    Clear criteria for each level and a guaranteed yearly conversation, so no one goes two years without a growth discussion.

    Owner: People team
  6. 3–6 months

    Rebuild the first 90 days

    An onboarding buddy outside the new hire’s reporting line, plus HR check-ins at 30, 60 and 90 days, so early problems with a manager or role surface before the person leaves.

    Owner: Hiring managers + Talent Acquisition

How I did it

  1. Excel

    Checked and cleaned the data

    Found and fixed 15 duplicate records, 20 missing salaries, 25 inconsistent department names and 8 records with impossible dates, so every number after this point can be trusted.

  2. SQL

    Answered the business questions

    Broke attrition down by team, reason, tenure, workload, pay and manager, and built the risk score that flags who to talk to first.

  3. Excel

    Built the savings model

    A what-if model leadership can adjust: change the target rate or cost assumption and see the savings.

  4. Power BI

    Made it easy to monitor

    An interactive dashboard that turns a one-off analysis into a monthly HR review.

Does overtime affect who leaves? Group employees by overtime hours and compare what share of each group left in 2025.

SELECT CASE
         WHEN MonthlyOvertimeHours <= 10 THEN '0-10 hrs'
         WHEN MonthlyOvertimeHours <= 20 THEN '11-20 hrs'
         ELSE 'Over 20 hrs'
       END                                       AS OvertimeBand,
       COUNT(*)                                  AS Employees,
       ROUND(100.0 * SUM(LeftIn2025) / COUNT(*), 1) AS PctLeft
FROM   product_teams_2025
GROUP  BY OvertimeBand;
